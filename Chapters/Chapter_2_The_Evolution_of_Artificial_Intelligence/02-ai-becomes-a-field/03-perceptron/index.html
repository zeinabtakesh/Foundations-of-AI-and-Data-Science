    The Perceptron

    1957–58 · Learning from examples

    The McCulloch–Pitts neuron showed how a simple unit could apply a fixed rule. The perceptron added something new: its connections could change with training.

    ← Timeline

    What is it?

    A perceptron is an early model of a learning machine developed by Frank Rosenblatt. A simple perceptron gives each input a weight, adds the weighted inputs, and produces a yes-or-no answer. Rosenblatt's Mark I Perceptron was a larger physical system built around this learning idea.

    How it works

    During training, the perceptron makes a prediction, checks the correct answer, and adjusts its weights when it is wrong. For example, when shown a letter it misidentifies, feedback changes its connections so a similar pattern may be classified differently next time.

    Key insights

    The earlier McCulloch–Pitts neuron followed connections and a threshold set by its designer; it did not learn from mistakes. The perceptron's added value was that feedback could change its weights, changing how it classified future inputs. It was an early step from programming a rule toward learning a rule from examples.

    A single-layer perceptron still cannot learn every kind of pattern. We will return to that limitation later in the chapter.

    Next: Samuel's checkers program, another early example of a machine improving with experience.

    More about Frank Rosenblatt

    Frank Rosenblatt (1928–1971) was an American psychologist at Cornell who studied how perception and learning might work in machines. He developed the perceptron model and led the work on its Mark I hardware.
