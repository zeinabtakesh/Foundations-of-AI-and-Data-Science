# The First AI Winter

**1970s · Expectations meet harder problems**

## What was the first AI winter?

An **AI winter** is a cooling of enthusiasm and funding after ambitious promises go unmet. The first is associated with the **1970s**.

## Why did confidence fall?

Early systems worked in controlled settings but struggled with complex real-world tasks. In the **1973 Lighthill report**, [James Lighthill](#james-lighthill){ data-open-details } argued that, as a problem grew, the number of possibilities a program had to consider could grow too quickly. This [**combinatorial explosion**](#combinatorial-explosion){ data-open-details } made small demonstrations poor evidence that AI could build a general-purpose intelligent robot. He saw more promise in narrower automation and computer-based studies of the mind. The report contributed to reduced support for broad AI research in Britain; US funders also shifted toward practical applications.

## Key insights

**Small success did not yet scale to real-world intelligence.** When ambitious claims outpaced results, some funders withdrew support. Researchers kept working, especially on more focused problems, so the “winter” was a cooling of confidence, not the end of AI.

<details class="turing-more" id="combinatorial-explosion" markdown>
<summary>What is combinatorial explosion?</summary>

If a program has **3 choices at each step**, then 2 steps create `3 × 3 = 9` possible paths, while 10 steps create `3¹⁰ = 59,049`. The number grows so fast that checking every path may work for a small example but take too long for a realistic problem.

</details>
