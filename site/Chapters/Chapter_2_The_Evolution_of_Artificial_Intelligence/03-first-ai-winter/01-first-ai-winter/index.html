    The First AI Winter

    1970s · Expectations meet harder problems

    Early AI demonstrations raised high expectations. By the 1970s, progress on real-world problems was slower than promised.

    ← Timeline

    What was the first AI winter?

    An AI winter is a cooling of enthusiasm and funding after ambitious promises go unmet. The first is associated with the 1970s.

    Why did confidence fall?

    Early systems worked in controlled settings but struggled with complex real-world tasks. In the 1973 Lighthill report, James Lighthill argued that, as a problem grew, the number of possibilities a program had to consider could grow too quickly. This combinatorial explosion made small demonstrations poor evidence that AI could build a general-purpose intelligent robot. He saw more promise in narrower automation and computer-based studies of the mind. The report contributed to reduced support for broad AI research in Britain; US funders also shifted toward practical applications.

    Key insights

    Small success did not yet scale to real-world intelligence. When ambitious claims outpaced results, some funders withdrew support. Researchers kept working, especially on more focused problems, so the “winter” was a cooling of confidence, not the end of AI.

    What is combinatorial explosion?

    If a program has 3 choices at each step, then 2 steps create 3 × 3 = 9 possible paths, while 10 steps create 3¹⁰ = 59,049. The number grows so fast that checking every path may work for a small example but take too long for a realistic problem.

    James Lighthill

    James Lighthill (1924–1998) was a British mathematician known for work in fluid dynamics. Asked to assess AI research in Britain, he wrote the influential report published in 1973.
